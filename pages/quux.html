The CADR, evolved

QUUX

QUUX is the CADR Lisp Machine changed a step at a time: the same processor, buses and microcode structure, with each change made where the profile shows the machine spends its time. muir models both, side by side: --machine cadr, MIT’s machine as built, and --machine quux.

The manual’s QUUX muir
クックス

I’m still CADR inside. QUUX keeps my microinstruction, my two buses and my way of running Lisp, and changes the parts that held me back.

Three projects make it: muir models each change and holds it to tests; muir-sys writes the microcode and builds the Lisp system for it; muir-fpga builds it in an FPGA. The CADR stays exactly MIT’s machine, running MIT’s microcode 323 and System 100; QUUX runs its own microcode, 1000, and System 1002.

What

What QUUX changes

Each change is a revision of the hardware, told to software by a word only QUUX answers, its MACHINE-ID, and a page of Xbus I/O space that lists its sizes. The measurements are muir’s own, over its profile workloads on System 1001.

The map

63

regions mapped at once, against 31

A six-bit level-1 map entry, carried on two bits the CADR left spare. The map was the largest single cost the profile found. 11% fewer microcycles overall.

The PDL buffer

16K

words, against 1K

The stack cache the processor keeps its frames in. Deep recursion stops spilling to memory: 29% fewer microcycles on the deep-recursion workload.

Multiply and divide

1

instruction each, against 32 and 33 steps

A multiply in one microcycle and a divide held for 330 ns, each bit for bit what the CADR’s step sequence gives. 20% fewer microcycles a macroinstruction on the multiply-and-divide workload.

A processor tick

60 Hz

the clock, in the processor

The CADR’s clock was its display’s vertical interrupt. QUUX has a tick of its own, with a period in microseconds, costing nothing until it rises.

MONO TV

1280×1024

one bit a pixel, any size

A plain frame buffer in place of the CADR’s TV boards, their sync program and their interrupt. The system reads the screen’s size from the hardware’s own page of sizes.

Block-disk

228

blocks, addressed by number

The CADR disk controller’s registers and command lists, with the drive’s cylinders, heads and sectors taken out: read and write a numbered block. Built for an SD card.

How fast

A 40 ns microcycle and a cache

The CADR takes a microcycle as a string of delay-line phases, 145 ns at its normal speed, and waits the better part of half a microsecond for every memory word. QUUX runs each microcycle in one short fixed cycle, four ticks of the FPGA’s 10 ns clock, and puts a 16 KB cache in front of memory. Nothing the microcode sees changes but the time.

Ten workloads, in the machine’s own timeSecondsFaster

That’s the same microcode doing the same work. The memory times are measured on the two FPGA boards; the rest is muir’s model of them.

These are modeled, not yet run: muir-fpga’s fits put the processor’s longest path at 37 ns on the Arty and 20 ns on the DE25-Nano, and the synchronous core and the cache are theirs to build next. A cache of 4K words, 16 KB, is where the gain levels off: 64K words buys almost nothing more.

Do it

Running QUUX in muir

--machine quux chooses it, with its own boot PROM and MONO TV; --timing-model sync gives it the 40 ns microcycle and --cache 4096 the cache, on the rtl engine. It needs microcode written for it, from muir-sys: MIT’s own will not run on it. Every difference, what each reached in the microcode and the Lisp system, and what is still unverified, is in the manual’s QUUX.
