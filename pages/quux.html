QUUX

QUUX is the CADR, evolved: the same microinstruction and the same way of running Lisp, with the processor, its memory and its devices rebuilt where a change pays for itself. muir models both, side by side, as two executables: cadr, MIT’s machine as built, and quux.

Under development. QUUX is not finished: its changes land one at a time, and what this page describes may still change.

I’m still CADR inside. QUUX keeps my microinstruction and my way of running Lisp, and changes the parts that held me back.

Three projects make it: muir models each change and holds it to tests; muir-sys writes the microcode and builds the Lisp system for it; muir-fpga is the machine on an FPGA board, where the same flag chooses it. The CADR stays MIT’s machine; QUUX runs muir-sys’s microcode 1000 and System 1002.

Drawn

The CADR and QUUX

The CADR puts everything behind its bus interface: main memory, the disk and the display on the Xbus, and the keyboard, mouse, clocks and network on the Unibus through the I/O board. QUUX’s processor has its clocks inside, reaches main memory and the frame buffer on a port of its own through a cache, and its devices’ registers through its register decode; the devices move bulk data to and from memory themselves. There is no Unibus, no bus interface and no debug cable.

What

What QUUX changes

QUUX reports its hardware revision, 1 to 8, in a word only it answers, its MACHINE-ID; each revision contains the last. Software reads that word first, then a page of device registers that lists the machine’s sizes.

The map

63

regions mapped at once, against 31

A six-bit level-1 map entry where the CADR’s is five: 64 blocks of level-2 entries, 2,048 in all. The sixth bit travels on two bits the CADR leaves spare.

Measured: 11% fewer microcycles over thirteen workloads, 31% fewer interning symbols, 27% scrolling the listener, 21% compiling.

The PDL buffer

16K

words, against 1K

Its pointer and index are 14 bits where the CADR’s are 10. It needs QUUX’s own boot PROM, and microcode that knows the size: MIT’s 323 does not run on it.

Measured: 4% fewer microcycles over thirteen workloads, 29% fewer in deep recursion.

Multiply and divide

1 and 10

microcycles, multiply and divide, against 32 steps each

ALU functions 42 and 43, each bit for bit what the CADR’s step sequence gives. A multiply takes one microcycle; a divide ten: its own and nine held, counted from when its operands are ready.

Measured: 20% fewer microcycles a macroinstruction multiplying and dividing, 7% in floating point.

Clocks in the processor

60 Hz

at reset, in the processor

The CADR’s clock is its display’s vertical interrupt, and its microsecond clock and interval timer are on the I/O board. QUUX’s processor has all three: a fixed 60 Hz tick and an interval timer, part of the interrupt the microcode already tests, and a microsecond clock read in one microinstruction.

MONO TV

1280×1024

one bit a pixel, by default

A plain frame buffer and one register in place of the CADR’s TV boards: no sync program, no color map, no interrupt. It can be any size up to 1920 by 1080, and System 1002 sizes its screen from the feature page.

Block-disk

228

blocks, addressed by number

The CADR disk controller’s registers and command lists with the drive’s cylinders, heads and sectors taken out: read or write a numbered block of 256 words. It is QUUX’s only disk, and muir-sys’s boot PROM, microcode and System 1002 boot from it.

No Unibus

0

Unibus cycles, and no I/O board

The keyboard and mouse, the Chaosnet interface and the interrupt and error status moved to QUUX’s register page and the clocks into the processor; the serial port was dropped, and the Unibus went with them. The debug cable, a Unibus master, went too: the console’s registers are the host’s port alone.

The memory port

4K

words of cache, always

Main memory is off the Xbus it shared with the devices: the processor reaches it, and the frame buffer, on a port of its own through a write-through cache, a line fill no sooner than 380 ns and a write 290 ns, the slower of the two FPGA boards’ memories. The CADR’s bus interface has nothing left to do, and QUUX has none.

Device registers

2

microcycles a register, and no bus

The processor reaches its devices’ registers through its own register decode, at their old addresses: the display’s, block-disk’s and the register page. Registers carry control and small data; bulk data moves between a device and memory by itself. An address nothing answers fails at once, where the CADR’s Xbus waits microseconds for a timeout.

A standard disk

VHD

with a GPT partition table

QUUX’s disk is a fixed or dynamic VHD, partitioned by GPT: made with qemu-img, partitioned with gdisk, and filled with dd. A partition’s GPT name is its own four-character name, LOD4, a space and its comment; the current microcode and band are marked by attribute bits. The CADR keeps MIT’s pack and label.

How fast

A 40 ns microcycle and a cache

The CADR takes a microcycle as a string of delay-line phases, 145 ns at its normal speed, and its memory board answers a request 458 ns later. QUUX drops the delay lines: every microcycle is a fixed number of 10 ns ticks, four by default, and a cache stands in front of memory. Nothing the microcode sees changes but the time. QUUX’s own main memory is timed as the slower board’s, the last row.

That’s the same microcode doing the same work. The memory times are measured on the two FPGA boards; the rest is muir’s model of them.

Modeled in muir, on rtl, with System 1001 and muir-sys’s microcode for revision 4. Four ticks, 40 ns, meets timing on both boards in muir-fpga’s fits: the Arty Z7-20 with +0.461 ns to spare and the DE25-Nano with +0.180 ns. The cache’s 20 ns hit is met on the Arty in muir-fpga’s fit; the DE25’s to follow. Past 4K words the cache gains little: 16K words gives 3.90× where 4K gives 3.84×, both without the write buffer.

Running QUUX in muir

The quux executable runs it, with its own boot PROM, MONO TV and block-disk; its microcycle is 40 ns, always, and on the rtl engine its cache and main memory are timed as the slower board’s. Its disk is a VHD with a GPT partition table. It needs microcode and a band written for it, from muir-sys: MIT’s own will not run on it. Every difference, what each reached in the boot PROM, the microcode and the Lisp system, and what is still unverified, is in the manual’s QUUX.
